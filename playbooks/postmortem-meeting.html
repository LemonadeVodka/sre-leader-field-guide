Running the Postmortem Meeting

Your playbook as the senior manager: before the meeting, your script for the 45 minutes, handling the room, the decisions you own, what happens after, and the variants.

Quick Card: SAFER

  1. S Set the rules: blameless, facts first, owned actions. Copy them.
  2. A Agree the impact: who, how long, and how did we find out?
  3. F Follow the timeline: what did you know then, and what made sense?
  4. E Explain what let it happen: trigger, what made us vulnerable, why not caught sooner?
  5. R Rank the actions: if only one, which? Owner, date, and what done means.

Use This When

You're sponsoring or running a postmortem (RCA review) meeting. The RCA section covers writing and judging the document. This playbook covers running the room: what you do before, what you say during, how you handle the hard moments, and what you own afterwards.

Before the Meeting

From two days before, up to the start of the meeting:

  • The draft passes the 5-minute check. If it doesn't, send it back with the feedback reply. A weak draft wastes 45 minutes of eight people's time.
  • The right people are invited:
    • the author
    • the incident lead
    • the NOC analyst who saw the first alert
    • an engineer from the owning pod and the pod manager, who can commit to dates
    • SRE on-call
    • product, if customers were affected
  • The pre-read goes out 24 hours before. Ask everyone to read it before the meeting, not in it.
  • Talk privately first to anyone whose change triggered the incident ("You'll hear your change discussed. Here's how we'll frame it: the system let it through."), and to the manager of a pod that's likely to be defensive.
  • Name a facilitator and a note-taker, so you can listen.

Handling the Room

When…SayWhy it works
Blame emerges ("Sam pushed a bad change")"Let's hold the who. What let that change reach every user?"It moves the room to the system without shaming anyone. That's where the real fixes are.
Someone gets defensive"Nobody's on trial. If you'd had a canary, would this have been caught?"It gives them a way to contribute to the fix instead of defending themselves.
A senior engineer dominates"Thanks, let me get [quieter person]'s view. You were closest to the first alert."It brings out the people with the most first-hand information.
It gets too technical for you"I'm going to translate. So users saw X because Y, and the fix is Z? Is that right?"It keeps you leading, and makes the RCA readable for everyone else.
An exec asks "who did this?""The change came from the pod, but the real question is why our process let it reach everyone. Here are the three things we're fixing."It protects the person and redirects the exec to systemic fixes, which is what they actually need.
The NOC analyst stays silentAsk directly and warmly: "What did you see at 02:10, and what did the runbook tell you to do?"Offshore L1 staff often won't volunteer in a senior meeting. A direct, specific question makes it safe.
People disagree on the cause"What evidence would settle it? Let's take it offline, and record both until we know."It stops a debate that eats the actions block.
A pod refuses an action"Understood. Let's capture it as an open risk with your name on the decision, and I'll discuss priority with [pod manager] this week."It makes the refusal visible and owned, and moves the negotiation to the right level.
You're running out of time"Let's lock the top action with an owner and date now. I'll finish the rest async by Friday."The one action that matters never gets lost.

Decisions You Own

  • Accept, rank, or reject actions. You can say "that's too vague" or "that won't prevent this."
  • Which actions need a manager-to-manager priority conversation, and holding it within a week.
  • Who it's shared with: other pods, the NOC, support, leadership.
  • Whether leadership needs a readout, using the 5-minute script below.

Repeat-Incident Review

Use the full 45-minute agenda, but spend the trigger and factors block on one question: "We said we'd do X last time. Why didn't it happen, or why didn't it work?" The usual answers are a priority conflict, an action that wasn't specific enough, or an owner who left. The fix is usually a manager-level decision, so bring the pod manager.

Example in Practice

Minute 12, timeline walk. A pod lead says: "Honestly, the NOC sat on this for 19 minutes." The NOC analyst goes quiet.

You: "Let's hold the who. [Analyst], what did the runbook tell you to do when the alert fired?" The answer: "Monitor for 15 minutes, then escalate." You: "So the NOC followed the runbook exactly. The runbook was the delay. Who owns fixing it?" The pod lead nods, and the NOC lead takes the action with a one-week date.

In other words, one redirect turned a blame moment into the incident's cheapest fix, and the offshore team saw you defend them in front of a pod.

What Actually Happens

The meeting becomes a re-debug of the incident.
Counter: "Good question for after. Let's finish the timeline." Keep the agenda visible on screen.
People attend without reading the pre-read.
Counter: start with a 3-minute silent read. People adjust quickly.
Actions get agreed in the room, then quietly deprioritized.
Counter: the pod manager is in the room, actions go in the tracker within a day, and the monthly review checks them.
The same people dominate every review.
Counter: always ask the person closest to the first alert to start the timeline.
Execs want a name.
Counter: give them the systemic answer and three actions. They mostly want to know it won't happen again.
