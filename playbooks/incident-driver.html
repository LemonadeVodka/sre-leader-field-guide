Reliability Field Guide

Move a Stuck Incident

Questions that keep an incident moving when the NOC can't, organized by how it's stuck. You don't need to know the fix; you need to know what to ask next.

Quick Card: LEAD, Every 15 Minutes

  1. L Leader: "Who's leading this?" If nobody: "[Name], can you lead until the owning team joins? I'll handle exec updates."
  2. E Effect: "What are users seeing, how many, since when, and is it getting worse?"
  3. A Action: "What's ruled out, what's our best guess, and what's the safest thing we can do in the next 15 minutes?"
  4. D Deadline: "Who are we waiting on, when do we escalate, and when's the next update?"

Nobody Owns It

Five people investigating with nobody deciding is the most common reason an incident stays stuck. Naming a leader is the single highest-value thing you can do, and it needs no technical knowledge.

Impact Is Unclear

If nobody can say who is affected, the team can't judge urgency, pick a severity, or tell whether a change helped.

Going in Circles

Write down what's ruled out, so it stops being re-checked. Then turn the next check into a decision: "if we see X, we do Y." To walk the hops in order, use the Request Map.

No Theory Yet

A wrong theory you test beats no theory at all. Ask for a bet, and what would prove it wrong. Not sure where to point? Use the Request Map.

Waiting on Someone

A Slack message is not a page. Get a name, a time, an acknowledgement, and a backup with a deadline. Escalate on a clock, not on a feeling.

Manager-to-manager escalation (send at the deadline)
Hi [name], we have a Sev [x] on [service] affecting [impact] since [time].
Your on-call was paged at [time] and we don't have [an acknowledgement / a path forward] yet.
Can you get someone from your team on the bridge in the next 10 minutes? Link: [bridge / channel]

Afraid to Act

Teams freeze because acting feels risky and waiting feels safe. Make the cost of waiting visible, then get a decision made by the right person.

The NOC Is Lost

When the NOC is relaying messages they don't understand, slow down for one minute: get what they've actually seen and done, then give one clear next step. Kind, specific, and firm.

One clear instruction to the NOC
[Name], here's your next step:
1. Page [team] on-call in PagerDuty now.
2. Paste this in the incident channel: impact, start time, what you've tried, link to the graph.
3. Tell me on the bridge when they acknowledge.
Don't restart anything else until the owning team joins.

Exec Heat

Take the comms so the engineers can keep fixing. One person, on a schedule, with honest uncertainty.

15-minute exec update
[Service] Sev [x]: [what users are seeing], since [time].
Now: [what we're trying]. Owner: [team / name].
Confidence: [low / medium / high] that this fixes it.
Next update: [time], or sooner if it changes.

The 15-Minute Rhythm

Once it's moving, keep it moving. Every 15 minutes, out loud on the bridge:

MinutesAsk
1 minWhat changed? Better, worse, or flat since the last check.
1 minWhat's next? One action, one owner, and when we'll know.
1 minWho are we waiting on? Anyone past their deadline gets escalated now.
1 minIs the severity still right, and is the exec update sent?
4 minTotal, every 15 minutes

If nothing has improved for 60 minutes, it's a business decision, not a technical one. Use the Stuck Incident deep dive for escalation packets, pre-approved levers, and getting your team out of surface-only.

Example in Practice

Twenty Minutes of Nothing, Then Movement

The assistant is timing out for about a third of users. The NOC opened the incident and is relaying alert text. Four engineers are on the bridge, all looking at the same dashboard.

You ask who's leading. Silence. "Marco, can you lead until the platform team joins? I'll handle exec updates." You ask what users are seeing: "a third of requests fail, since 09:40, getting worse." You ask for a bet: "the model provider; our servers look fine." You ask who's checking it: nobody. One engineer takes it. You ask who we're waiting on: platform was "pinged in Slack." You page them properly and set a 10-minute deadline. At 09:58 platform joins, confirms provider throttling, and switches traffic to the backup region. You send the exec update and hand over IC.

You didn't diagnose anything. You named a leader, got the impact clear, turned a guess into a check, and escalated on a clock.

What Actually Happens

  • People will wait for you to fix it. Don't try. Keep asking who owns the next step.
  • Someone will say "we're close" for an hour. Ask what result would prove it, and set a time.
  • The NOC will go quiet when you take over. Give them a job (watching the error graph, posting updates) so they stay involved and learn.
  • An engineer may push back on you leading. "Great, take IC. I'll handle comms." Either way, someone now leads.
