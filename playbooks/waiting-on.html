When They Say "I'm Waiting On…"

Six kinds of waiting, the confident first line for each, and how to keep work moving without chasing it yourself.

Quick Card: When You Hear "I'm Waiting On…"

  1. "Who exactly, since when, and what did you ask for?"
  2. "While you wait, what can you move forward today?"
  3. "Can we split it so the part that doesn't depend on them ships now?"
  4. "Can you add a 'blocked by' link in Linear and a date you'll follow up?"
  5. "If there's no answer by [day], tell me and I'll raise it with their manager."

Use This When

An engineer says "I'm waiting on platform," "I'm waiting on approval," or "I'm waiting on a decision," and you want to sound confident and help them move, without taking the chasing on yourself. The trick: name the kind of wait. Each kind has a different first question and a different fix.

Six Kinds of Waiting

A person or an answer"Who exactly, since when, and what did you ask for?"The engineer re-asks with a specific deadline. Often the first ask was vague.No answer by the deadline: you message their manager.
A decision"Whose decision is it? Let's get it on their calendar this week."The engineer writes two options and a recommendation; you get the decision-maker to choose.Decisions are yours to unblock. Don't let one wait more than a week.
Access or approval"What's the ticket, and who approves it?"Link the ticket in Linear. Check it has everything the approver needs.Older than 5 working days: manager to manager.
Another team's work"Is it on their board, with a date?"Linear "blocked by" link to their issue. If it's not on their board, it isn't happening.No date from them: their manager. Still nothing: the risk register.
A spec or information"What's the smallest assumption we can make and move on?"Write the assumption in the issue. Confirm it later. Build what you can.If the assumption is risky, it's a decision: see above.
A vendor or partner"What's their response time in the contract, and when did we ask?"Use the vendor contact, not a general support form.Past their contracted time: vendor management, or log a risk.

Always Ask These Two

  • "While you wait, what can you move forward today?" This keeps people productive and makes "waiting" a status, not a day off.
  • "Can we split it so the part that doesn't depend on them ships now?" Most blocked work has a piece that isn't blocked.

Sound Confident, Not Anxious

Confidence comes from structure: state it, ask one specific question, set a date. Compare:

Sounds anxiousSounds confident
"Any update on that? No pressure…""[Issue] is waiting on platform since Monday. What's the next step, and by when?"
"Hmm, OK, let me know when you hear back.""Follow up Thursday. If there's no answer, tell me and I'll raise it with their manager."
"Should we maybe escalate? I don't know…""It's been a week. I'm taking it to Jordan today."
"I'll chase them for you.""You own the follow-up; I own the escalation if it stalls."
"It's probably fine.""What would make your date confidence a 9?"

Make Waiting Visible in Linear

  1. Every blocked issue gets a "blocked by" link to the issue it waits on (even in another team's workspace or project).
  2. Add a comment with who, since when, what was asked, and the follow-up date.
  3. Use a Blocked status or label consistently, so your weekly sweep can filter it.
  4. Review blocked issues every week in your Linear sweep.
Blocked comment (paste into the Linear issue)
Blocked by: [linked issue or team]
Waiting on: [person], since [date]
Asked for: [the specific thing]
Meanwhile: [what we're moving forward]
Follow up: [date]. If no answer, escalate to [manager].

When to Stop Chasing

Chasing more than three times isn't persistence; it's a sign the wait needs a decision. Move it up:

  1. Engineer follows up with a deadline.
  2. You message the other team's manager (one message, specific ask, date).
  3. Still stuck: send a "fix, fund, or accept" request and log it on the risk register.
Manager-to-manager message
Hi [manager], quick one: [engineer] has been waiting since [date] on [specific thing] from your team ([link]).
It's blocking [what], which matters because [impact].
Could someone on your side give us a date by [day]? If it can't happen this cycle, that's OK, I just need to know so we can plan around it.

Saying "It's Late" Upward

Late-project note to your VP
[Project] will be late. New date: [date].
Why: waiting on [team] for [thing] since [date]; escalated to [manager] on [date].
Meanwhile: [what moved anyway].
Need from you: [nothing / a decision / a word with their director].

Example in Practice

Three 'Waiting On's in One Standup

Marco: "Waiting on platform for a service account." You: "Ticket number, and since when?" Two weeks. "That's past five days; I'll message Jordan today. Meanwhile, can you finish the runbook part?" Lin: "Waiting on product for the alert threshold." You: "That's a decision. Can you write two options and your pick by tomorrow? I'll get product to choose Thursday." Tomás: "Waiting on the vendor's API docs." You: "What's the smallest assumption we can make and build against?" He picks one, writes it in the issue, and keeps going.

Three different waits, three different moves. Nobody left with "OK, let me know."

What Actually Happens

  • "Waiting on" is sometimes "stuck and embarrassed." Ask "what's the next step?" kindly; sometimes they don't know it.
  • Some teams never answer. That's what the risk register and the decision request are for.
  • You'll want to chase it yourself. Don't. Own the escalation, not the follow-up.
