When an Incident Is Stuck

The deep dive for a surface-only team: when you can see the problem but can't diagnose or fix it yet. Surface well, escalate on a clock, pull pre-approved levers, and build your way out.

Quick Card: Stuck and Nobody Here Can Fix It

  1. Declare and scope: "What are users seeing, how many, since when?"
  2. Get the owner in: page the owning team in PagerDuty with the escalation packet below.
  3. Escalate on a clock: T+15 no acknowledgement, PagerDuty escalates. T+30 no progress, call their manager. T+60, director level.
  4. Pull a pre-approved lever if one exists: failover, rollback, feature flag, rate limit.
  5. Own the comms every 15 minutes so the fixers can work.

Need the questions? Use Move a Stuck Incident. Fix stuck with another team? Risk register.

Use This When

An incident is open, users are affected, and nobody on the bridge can diagnose or fix it. Your team can see the problem and raise it, but the access, tools, and knowledge to fix it sit with a pod, a platform team, or a vendor. This is common for central SRE teams early on. This page is how to lead well inside that constraint, and how to get out of it.

Your Real Job When You Can't Fix It

"We can't fix it" is a normal state for a surface-only team today. It's not a personal failure. What you can own, and should be excellent at:

  • Detect and declare early. Open the PagerDuty incident against the written severity criteria. Don't wait for certainty.
  • Scope the impact. Who's affected, how many, since when, and what they see.
  • Gather evidence so the fixing team starts at minute 10, not minute 0.
  • Get the right owner in fast, and escalate on a clock, not on a feeling.
  • Coordinate and communicate: one channel, one incident lead, updates on schedule.
  • Pull any pre-approved levers you've been given, such as turning a feature off or switching to a fallback.

Done well, this is visible, valued work. Engineers will remember who handed them a clean escalation packet at 3 a.m.

The Stuck Clock

An escalation ladder with time triggers, so nobody has to decide in the moment whether it's "time to escalate." Agree these times with pod leadership before the next incident, and build the first steps into PagerDuty escalation policies.

Minutes since declaredActionHow
T+0Declare and page the owning podSeverity set by the criteria. Escalation packet posted.PagerDuty incident on the pod's service. Packet pinned in the channel.
T+15No acknowledgement: escalate automaticallyThe pod's secondary, then their manager.PagerDuty escalation policy. No human decision needed.
T+30No progress: manager to managerYou call the pod's manager directly.Phone or a direct message. Use the message below.
T+60Still stuck: director or exec levelThe pod names its own incident lead.The exec update below. Ask for a decision, not sympathy.
T+90Business decisionRun degraded, tell customers, or accept the risk explicitly.An exec decides, and it's written in the incident record.

The times are starting points. A Sev 1 might compress them, and a Sev 3 might skip the last two steps.

Mitigation Without Writing Code: Pre-Approved Levers

Most stuck time is spent waiting for permission, not for a fix. Agree in advance, per critical service, which levers SRE (or the NOC, with a runbook) may pull without waiting for the pod:

LeverWhat it doesWho usually provides it
Turn off a feature flagRemoves a new or risky feature without a deployPod (flag tool, or Azure App Configuration)
Roll back the last releaseReturns to the last known-good versionPod pipeline, with a one-click rollback job for SRE
Switch to a fallback modelRoutes AI requests to a backup model or providerPod (routing config)
Fail over a regionMoves traffic away from an unhealthy regionPlatform team (for example, Front Door rules)
Scale outAdds capacity for load-related failuresPlatform team (autoscale limits)
Shed non-critical workTurns off nice-to-have features to protect the core journeyPod (flags)
Pre-approved levers agreement (one per service)
Service: [name]            Owning pod: [name]       Pod manager: [name]
Agreed on: [date]          Review: every quarter, and after any incident where a lever was used

1. [Lever, e.g. turn off flag "assistant-new-tools"]   How: [runbook link]   Undo: [how]
2. [Lever, e.g. run rollback job for the last release] How: [runbook link]   Undo: [how]
3. [Lever, e.g. route to fallback model]             How: [runbook link]   Undo: [how]

SRE will: post in the incident channel before and after pulling a lever, and page the pod at the same time.

When Nobody Can Fix It Yet

  • Decide the degraded mode. What's the best experience you can give users now: a shorter answer, no tools, a clear "try again later"?
  • Communicate outward. Brief support, and decide on a status page update. Use the exec comms questions.
  • Make risk acceptance explicit. If the fix will take hours or days, an exec decides to accept the impact. Write it in the incident record.
  • Keep the rhythm. Updates every 30–60 minutes, even when the update is "no change, next update at 15:00."
  • Protect people. Rotate whoever has been on the bridge longest. See long-running incidents.

Getting Out of Surface-Only: A 90-Day Plan

The full program, with the Workbook background, pitches, and templates, is in Implementation: Ownership, On-Call & Engagement.

WhenDo this
Days 1–30Ownership map: every production service has an owning pod, a PagerDuty service, and an escalation policy. Agree the stuck clock with pod managers. Start measuring the two numbers below.
Days 31–60Access and levers: SRE gets read access to Application Insights, Log Analytics, and Grafana for every critical service. Sign pre-approved lever agreements for the top 3 services.
Days 61–90First-15-minutes runbooks: each critical service's pod writes a short "what to check first" runbook that SRE and the NOC can run. Add both to the production readiness review for new services.

What Done Looks Like

  • Every critical service has an owner and a PagerDuty route. Nobody spends 16 minutes asking "who owns this?"
  • SRE can see the telemetry for every critical service.
  • At least 3 services have signed lever agreements and tested runbooks.
  • Time to owner engaged (from SRE engaged to the owning team acknowledging) is measured and trending down.
  • Incidents where SRE lacked access is counted monthly, and reported to leadership as a risk.

Those two numbers are your case for change. "Last month, 6 of 9 incidents stalled an average of 34 minutes waiting for an owner, and in 4 we had no access to the telemetry" is an argument leadership can act on. See Owning the Reliability Story.

Example in Practice

14:12: the NOC raises slow assistant answers. SRE confirms the model provider is throttling calls: 18% of requests are failing. SRE can't change the routing or the prompt. Both belong to the Assistant pod.

14:15: SRE posts the escalation packet, with the Grafana panel, an App Insights query showing the throttled calls with two example IDs, and a deploy annotation at 13:52 for a prompt change. The pod is paged.

14:30: the pod hasn't acknowledged, and PagerDuty has already escalated to their manager. You call the manager directly and send the T+30 message. The pod's engineer joins at 14:38 and starts from the packet instead of from scratch.

14:45: the pod confirms a rollback will take 25 minutes. You ask: "Is there a fallback model we can switch to now?" There is, and it's pre-approved. SRE switches it at 14:47, and errors drop to 2% while the rollback runs.

In other words, SRE didn't fix anything, but the incident was mitigated 40 minutes sooner because of the packet, the clock, and a lever agreed in advance.

What Actually Happens

The pod says "it's not us."
Counter: "Then help us prove it in 10 minutes. Here's the packet. If it's not you, who would you page?" Ownership disputes get resolved after the incident, in the review. During it, the default owner is whoever owns the user-facing service.
Ping-pong: pod → platform → provider → pod.
Counter: name one incident lead from the owning pod who keeps it until it's handed over explicitly. The incident is never "with nobody."
Everyone watches, nobody acts.
Counter: "What's the next decision, and who's making it?" Then set a 15-minute checkpoint.
"We're waiting on the provider."
Counter: waiting isn't a plan. Ask: "What can we do on our side while we wait: fallback, degraded mode, comms?"
Execs get frustrated with SRE because "SRE owns incidents."
Counter: be explicit in every update about what SRE can and can't change, and who owns the fix. Then bring the two metrics to the next leadership review.
