Reliability Field Guide

Executive Communications During Incidents

One voice, on a clock, honest about uncertainty. Update templates for every moment, the hard questions execs ask and what to say, and the cadence by severity.

Quick Card: Exec Comms in One Breath

  1. One voice: you (or a named comms lead) send every update. Engineers keep fixing.
  2. On a clock: first notice within 10 minutes of declaring, then every 15–30 minutes, even if nothing changed.
  3. Four parts: impact in user terms · what we're doing · how confident we are · next update time.
  4. Honest uncertainty: "I don't know yet. Next update at [time]." Never a guessed ETA.
  5. Execs in a thread, not on the bridge.

Why This Is Your Job

During an incident, executive communication is where a senior manager adds the most visible value. Done well, it protects your engineers, keeps leaders calm, and builds the trust you'll need when you ask for budget, headcount, or controls. Done badly, it pulls engineers off the fix and makes every incident feel worse than it is.

Update Templates, by Moment

Fill in the brackets. Keep each to four or five lines. Execs read the first line and the next update time.

1. First notice (within 10 minutes of declaring)
Sev [x]: [what users can't do], [how many / %], since [time].
What we know: [one line, or "investigating; no cause yet"].
Owner: [team]. Incident lead: [name]. I'm handling updates.
Next update: [time] (in 15-30 minutes).
2. Regular update (every 15–30 minutes)
Sev [x] update, [time]: [impact now: better / worse / same as last update].
Now: [what we're trying], owner [team].
Confidence this fixes it: [low / medium / high].
Next update: [time], or sooner if it changes.
3. Mitigated
Mitigated at [time]: [what changed for users, e.g. errors back under 1%].
Still at risk: [what could bring it back, e.g. running on the backup region].
We're watching until [time] before calling it resolved.
Next update: [time] or at resolution.
4. Resolved
Resolved at [time]. Impact: [who], [how many], [duration].
Cause so far: [one plain line; full analysis in the postmortem].
Follow-up: postmortem on [date], owner [name].
Thanks to [teams] for the response.
5. Next-day readout (five lines)
[Incident], [date]: [impact in one line].
Why: [the 2-3 factors that matter, in plain words].
What we've already done: [mitigation still in place].
What's next: postmortem [date]; [N] fixes expected, owners named there.
What I need from you: [nothing / a decision / support with another team].
6. Hand-off to support or customer comms
For customer-facing comms (not for internal detail):
What users experience: [plain description].
Since: [time]. Workaround: [if any].
Next update to you: [time]. Please don't share causes until confirmed.

Tone Rules

  • Short and factual. Four or five lines. No technical detail they'd need to ask about.
  • User impact first, in plain words: "a third of EU users can't get answers," not "5xx on the gateway."
  • Label uncertainty: "likely," "we think," "confirmed." Never a guess dressed as fact.
  • No blame, no names attached to causes.
  • Always a next update time, and keep it, even if the update is "no change."
  • Silence reads as chaos. A late update is worse than a boring one.

Example in Practice

Two Real Updates From Tuesday's Incident

22:00: "Sev 2, assistant: ~1 in 3 EU requests failing since 21:25 (worse at peak). Now: rolling back a retry change (Assistant pod) and reaching platform for the provider side. Confidence: medium that this improves it, low that it fully fixes it. Next update: 22:15."

Why it works: impact in user terms, two owners named, honest confidence, a time.

22:45: "Failed over EU traffic to the backup region at 22:41. Errors now under 1%. Users in EU see slightly slower responses until we move back. Status: mitigated, not resolved. Next update: 23:15."

Why it works: says what users feel now, separates mitigated from resolved, and sets the next time. See the full incident.

What Actually Happens

  • Execs will push for an ETA. Give the next decision time instead. Most accept it the second time.
  • Someone senior will message an engineer directly. Reply in the same thread: "I've got updates for you; next at [time]." Then tell the engineer to keep going.
  • You'll be tempted to wait for good news before updating. Don't. Send "no change" on time.
  • Over-optimism costs more than bad news. Say "medium confidence" when you mean it.
