SRE Leader's Field Guide

Build the Incident Program

Severity criteria the NOC can apply, clear roles, PagerDuty paging that runs itself, a comms cadence, and quarterly drills.

Why It Matters

  • For the business: most long incidents aren't technically hard. They're disorganized. A consistent incident process shortens every incident, including the ones nobody can predict.
  • For you: the incident process is the part of reliability you can own outright, even without owning code. Running it well is where non-coding SRE leaders earn the most credibility.

What the Workbook Says

  • Use a clear incident command structure: an incident commander who coordinates, an operations lead who works the problem, and a communications lead who handles updates. Separate roles keep work from colliding. (Workbook, Ch. 9: Incident Response (opens in a new tab))
  • Declare early. It's cheaper to declare and downgrade than to declare late. (SRE Book, Ch. 14 (opens in a new tab))
  • Keep a live incident state document so anyone joining, or the next shift, can catch up quickly.
  • Practice. Incident response is a skill. Drills and reviews make it routine before the real thing.
  • Close the loop with blameless postmortems and tracked actions. (Ch. 10: Postmortem Culture (opens in a new tab))

Reality Check

  • The NOC declares, but L1 can't judge "serious." Severity must be objective criteria, not judgment.
  • SRE often can't fix it. The process must include fast escalation to owners, and the stuck clock.
  • Time zones: long incidents cross shifts, so handoffs are part of the process, not an afterthought.
  • Execs join bridges. A communications role and cadence protects engineers from interruptions.

Before You Start

  • PagerDuty services exist for at least the critical services (see Ownership).
  • Your boss agrees to back the severity criteria and the comms cadence.
  • The NOC lead is a co-owner of the rollout.

How to Sell It

To your boss
Our incidents run long mainly because of coordination: who leads, who to page, who updates execs.
I want to roll out a lightweight incident program over 4 weeks: objective severity criteria the NOC can apply,
named roles, PagerDuty paging that runs itself, a comms cadence for execs, and quarterly drills.
Ask: your backing on the severity criteria, and that execs use the comms channel rather than pinging engineers mid-incident.
To pod managers
We're standardizing incidents: clear severity, one incident lead, and an escalation ladder with times.
For you that means fewer vague pages, a clean escalation packet when we do page, and fewer execs going straight to your engineers.
What we need: your PagerDuty service and escalation policy kept current, and agreement on the ladder times.

Copy-Ready Artifacts

Severity criteria (starting point)
Sev 1: Core journey (e.g. ask the assistant) failing or unusable for >= 25% of users, OR any data/security exposure.
        Page owning pod + SRE immediately. Updates every 30 min. Exec notified.
Sev 2: Core journey degraded (slow, errors 5-25%) OR a major feature down for a segment, > 15 minutes.
        Page owning pod + SRE. Updates every 60 min.
Sev 3: Minor feature degraded, workaround available, or small segment affected.
        Ticket + business-hours follow-up. No exec updates.
When unsure: declare the higher severity. Downgrading later is free.

How You'll Know It's Working

  • Leading: severity criteria used in every incident; a named lead within 10 minutes; updates on schedule; drills held.
  • Lagging: time to mitigate falling; time to owner engaged falling; fewer exec escalations to engineers; RCAs on time.

What Actually Happens

Severity gets argued in the moment.
Counter: "Against the criteria, what's the severity?" If the criteria don't fit, fix them after the incident, not during.
Roles exist on paper; in practice everyone debugs.
Counter: the first question in every incident is "who's leading?" Drills build the habit.
Drills get cancelled for real work.
Counter: keep them to 60 minutes, put them in the calendar a quarter ahead, and bring the findings to leadership.
