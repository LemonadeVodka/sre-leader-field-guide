Fix Alerting & Paging

Cut alert noise, give every page a runbook and a clear NOC trigger, and move to SLO burn-rate alerts routed through PagerDuty.

Why It Matters

  • For the business: noisy alerts hide real ones. When on-call gets 40 pages a week, the important page at 3 a.m. gets the same tired response as the 39 that didn't matter.
  • For you: alert noise is the fastest way to burn out SRE and teach the offshore NOC to ignore alarms. Cutting it is a visible, early win that buys your team time and trust.

What the Workbook Says

  • Judge alerts on four qualities: precision (how many alerts are real), recall (how many real problems alert), detection time, and reset time. Any alert setup trades these off. (Workbook, Ch. 5: Alerting on SLOs (opens in a new tab))
  • Alert on SLO burn rate, meaning how fast the error budget is being used, not on raw thresholds. Fast burn pages a person; slow burn opens a ticket.
  • Use multiple time windows (for example a short and a long window together), so alerts fire quickly on big problems but don't flap on blips.
  • On-call load is a health metric. Track pages per shift, and treat sustained overload as a problem to fix, not something to endure. (Ch. 8: On-Call (opens in a new tab))

Reality Check

  • Alerts were added over time by different people, and nobody remembers why half of them exist. Expect to find duplicates and alerts that nobody acts on.
  • The NOC follows alerts literally. An alert without a clear runbook and escalation trigger creates escalation delays (see the worked example).
  • Alerts may live in two places, Grafana Alerting and Azure Monitor. Inventory both.
  • Deleting alerts feels risky to people who've been burned. Demote to a ticket or a dashboard first, then delete after 30 quiet days.

Before You Start

  • Access to PagerDuty reporting for the last 30–90 days.
  • A list of alert rules from Grafana Alerting and Azure Monitor.
  • The NOC lead and one SRE engineer committed for about 3 weeks.
  • At least one SLO in progress, for the burn-rate step (see SLOs From Zero).

How to Sell It

To your boss
On-call got [N] pages last month; about [X%] needed no action. That's noise burning out SRE and training the NOC to tune out alarms.
Over 4 weeks we'll cut noisy alerts, give every remaining page a runbook and a clear NOC trigger, and move to SLO-based alerts.
Target: under [2] pages per shift. Cost: one engineer part-time. Risk is managed: we demote alerts before we delete them.
To a pod manager
Your service's alerts paged us [N] times last month, mostly [alert]. We'd like 30 minutes with someone on your side
to agree which alerts matter and what the first response should be. You'll get fewer bogus escalations
from us and the NOC, and the ones you do get will come with evidence.

Good paging alert checklist
[ ] It's about user impact (or imminent user impact), not a cause like CPU at 80%
[ ] A human can do something about it right now
[ ] It links to a runbook with a clear STOP-and-escalate point
[ ] The NOC knows the objective trigger ("if X for Y minutes, page SRE")
[ ] It routes to the PagerDuty service of the team that can act
[ ] It paged no more than 5 times last month without a fix decision

How You'll Know It's Working

  • Leading: the inventory is done; the share of alerts with runbooks; the number of burn-rate alerts live.
  • Lagging: pages per shift falling; NOC time-to-escalate falling; fewer incidents found by customers first.

What Actually Happens

"We can't delete that, it caught something once in 2024."
Counter: demote it to a ticket or a dashboard. If it really matters, its SLO burn-rate alert will catch the user impact.
Noise creeps back within months.
Counter: the monthly page review and the "5 pages without action" rule stop the creep.
Burn-rate math confuses people.
Counter: explain it in one line: "we page if, at this rate, we'd use [X%] of the month's budget within the hour." The detail stays with SRE.
