Ownership, On-Call & Engagement Model

Your way out of surface-only: an ownership map, an escalation chain, SRE access, pre-approved levers, first-15-minutes runbooks, and a readiness gate.

Why It Matters

  • For the business: when ownership is unclear, every incident starts with a hunt for an owner. That's dead time with users affected.
  • For you: this is the way out of surface-only. A clear engagement model defines what SRE does, what pods do, and what SRE is allowed to do during an incident. It's also how you stop central SRE from becoming the dumping ground for every unowned problem.

What the Workbook Says

  • SRE engagement is deliberate, not automatic. Services are onboarded through an assessment such as a production readiness review, and the relationship has explicit terms. (Workbook, Ch. 18: SRE Engagement Model (opens in a new tab))
  • Shared goals, written down. SLOs act as the agreement between SRE and the development team about what "reliable enough" means.
  • Engagement can change over time. It can deepen, reduce, or even end when a service doesn't meet the bar. (SRE Book, Ch. 32 (opens in a new tab))
  • Sustainable on-call needs balanced load, good runbooks, and the authority to act. (Ch. 8: On-Call (opens in a new tab))

Reality Check

  • Today SRE and the NOC surface problems, while pods own the code, the access, and the fixes. Incidents stall in between. (See When an Incident Is Stuck.)
  • Pods may resist taking on-call for their own services. Frame it as "you share the pager for what only you can fix," not "SRE is pushing work back."
  • Access requests take time through security reviews. Start them in week 1.
  • AI components (prompts, models, tools, quota) often have unclear owners. Name them explicitly.

Before You Start

  • Your boss sponsors the program and agrees to raise it with pod leadership.
  • A list of production services, even rough, from Azure resources or App Insights.
  • Your recent incidents, with "time to owner engaged" estimated, for the business case.

How to Sell It

To your boss
Last month [X] of [Y] incidents stalled an average of [Z] minutes waiting for an owner, and in [N] SRE had no access to the telemetry.
I want a 90-day program: an ownership map with a PagerDuty route for every service, read access for SRE, pre-approved levers for the
top 3 services, and a readiness gate for new launches. Ask: you sponsor it with pod leadership, and we report both numbers monthly.
To pod managers
We want fewer 2 a.m. "is this yours?" calls for both of us. Three asks:
1) confirm your services and PagerDuty route in our ownership map,
2) agree a couple of pre-approved levers SRE can pull (with a runbook) so we can stop the bleeding before your engineer joins,
3) a short "first 15 minutes" runbook per critical service.
In return: cleaner escalations with evidence, and fewer pages to your team for things we can now handle.

How You'll Know It's Working

  • Leading: % of critical services fully mapped; % with SRE access; number of signed lever agreements.
  • Lagging: time to owner engaged falling; fewer stuck incidents; levers pulled in real incidents; new launches arriving with PRRs done.

What Actually Happens

Pods agree to the map but never update it.
Counter: review it quarterly in the PRR process, and after any incident where it was wrong.
Pods refuse pre-approved levers: "only we touch prod."
Counter: start with the safest lever (a feature flag off, with a runbook) for one service. One successful use builds trust for the next.
The PRR becomes a box-ticking exercise.
Counter: keep it short, focus it on on-call outcomes, and let SRE say "not yet" with your backing.
