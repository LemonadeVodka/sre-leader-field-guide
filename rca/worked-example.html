Worked Example: Assistant Timeouts at Peak

A fictional incident taken from raw evidence to a finished RCA, step by step, with the questions you'd ask at each stage.

About This Example

The incident is fictional, but realistic for a cross-device AI assistant running on Azure, with Grafana dashboards, PagerDuty paging, an offshore NOC, and a third-party model provider. It follows the eight steps in Running an RCA. Your questions at each step are in italics.

Step 1: Does It Need an RCA?

Yes. It was a Sev 2 with 83 minutes of customer-visible impact, and it was the second throttling-related incident in 60 days. The incident lead (Sam, SRE) writes the draft by Monday, and the review is booked for Wednesday.

You: "Sam, can you own the draft? Priya, can you join to cover the pod side? I've booked Wednesday at 10."

The kind of query an engineer ran (you don't need to write it)
// Application Insights / Log Analytics (KQL): throttled calls to the model provider, per minute
dependencies
| where timestamp between (datetime(2026-10-08 13:30) .. datetime(2026-10-08 15:45))
| where target has "model-provider"
| summarize throttled = countif(resultCode == "429"), total = count() by bin(timestamp, 1m)
| render timechart

You: "Where did 14:05 come from, the graph or someone's memory?" "Can we link the PagerDuty entry for the acknowledgement time?"

Step 3: Impact in User Terms

"From 14:05 to 15:28 (83 minutes), about 22% of assistant requests (about 180,000) failed or took over 30 seconds. Users saw 'Something went wrong. Try again.' All regions and device types were affected."

You: "Would support recognize this description from the tickets they got?"

Step 4: Trigger vs Conditions

  • Trigger (why now): the new prompt template sends about 40% more tokens per request, and it shipped just before the afternoon peak.
  • Contributing factors (why we were vulnerable):
    • Prompt templates are treated as config and skip the canary.
    • There's no alert on throttling or on token usage approaching the quota.
    • Clients retry 3 times immediately, without backoff, which makes throttling worse.
    • The NOC runbook says "monitor for 15 minutes."
    • Nobody had written down who owns the provider quota.

You: "If someone else had shipped a different long prompt, would we have had the same outage?" (Yes. So the problem is the conditions, not the person.)

Step 5: The Four Defenses

DefenseWhat failedWhat held
PreventNo canary for prompt changes. No token estimate before deploy.Not applicable
DetectNo throttling or quota alert, so we only saw the symptom.The latency alert fired within 7 minutes.
MitigateRetries without backoff amplified the problem and slowed recovery.Rollback worked in minutes once it was decided.
RespondThe NOC waited 15 minutes. Ownership of the quota was unclear.SRE acknowledged 3 minutes after the escalation.

You: "Every defense except detection failed somewhere. Do we have an action for each one?"

Step 6: 5 Whys, With Two Branches

  1. Why did requests fail? Provider calls were throttled.
  2. Why? We exceeded the token-per-minute quota at peak.
  3. Why? The new prompt uses about 40% more tokens, and it went to 100% of traffic at once.
  4. Why at once? Prompt changes skip the canary. → Prevent branch
  5. Why didn't we see the quota coming? We don't alert on token usage or throttling. → Detect branch

A third branch, "Why did it take 68 minutes to mitigate?", leads to the NOC runbook rule and the unclear ownership. That's the respond branch.

You: "If we only did one, which? Is #2 really a week? What does Priya need to hit three weeks?"

Step 8: Review and Follow-Through

  • Review: 45 minutes. The NOC analyst who took the first alert walked through the first 15 minutes, and they were thanked for following the runbook exactly as written.
  • Shared: a 5-line summary went to leadership, support, and the NOC.
  • Tracked: actions are on the monthly reliability review. Two weeks later, #2 and #3 are done. #2 was verified with a simulated throttle in staging.
  • Verified: the next prompt change went through the canary, hit throttling at 5% of traffic, and rolled back automatically. Users never noticed. Log that as a win.

What the Weak Version Would Have Said

"Root cause: an engineer deployed a prompt change without testing. Action: test prompt changes more carefully." That fixes nothing on the list above, and the next long prompt, from anyone, causes the same outage.
