SRE Leader's Field Guide

When It's Used on You

The destructive tactics people use at work, and how to recognize, counter, document, and escalate each one.

Use This When

Something feels off: your credibility is quietly eroding, decisions happen without you, or your team keeps getting blamed. These are the destructive tactics people use in organizations. They're here so you can recognize, counter, document, and escalate them. They aren't here for you to use: each one creates HR and legal exposure, and they end careers when discovered.

Three Rules for Any of These

  • Write it down, the same day: date, what was said or done, who was present, and the impact. Facts, not interpretations.
  • Respond in writing where possible. A calm written record beats a heated conversation.
  • Escalate on the pattern, not a single event. One incident might be a misunderstanding. Three is a pattern your boss or HR should see.

Gaslighting

Signs: you're told things you remember clearly didn't happen ("I never agreed to that date"). Your concerns get recast as overreactions. You start doubting your own records.

What it's for: avoiding accountability by making you unsure of the facts.

Counter: rely on written records. Follow every agreement with a recap: "Confirming what we agreed: X by Friday." When it's contradicted: "My notes from the 14th say X. Let's go with the written version."

Document: recaps, chat messages, meeting notes. Escalate when: it repeats, or it affects your team's standing.

Credit Theft

Signs: your team's fix or idea shows up in someone else's update or presentation, framed as theirs.

What it's for: visibility and promotion at your expense.

Counter: tell your story first and in writing: the weekly update, with names and dates. When it happens in a meeting: "Glad that landed. Ana on my team built it. Ana, want to add anything?" Calm, factual, public.

Document: dated evidence of the work (tickets, commits, messages). Escalate when: it's repeated, or affects reviews or promotions.

Scapegoating SRE or the NOC

Signs: after an outage, the story becomes "the NOC escalated late" or "SRE should have caught it," while the triggering change goes unmentioned.

What it's for: shifting blame away from the team that caused it, often toward the team with the least political protection.

Counter: insist on the evidence-based timeline and the blameless norm (see RCA the SRE Way). "The NOC followed the runbook exactly. The runbook was the delay. And the trigger was the change at 13:52." Protect your offshore team in public, every time.

Document: the PagerDuty timeline, Activity Log, and runbook versions. Escalate when: the blame story reaches leadership uncorrected.

Information Starvation

Signs: you learn about launches, decisions, or incidents late, or second-hand. You're left off distribution lists. Data requests go unanswered.

What it's for: weakening you by making you look uninformed.

Counter: ask explicitly to be on the lists. Build alternative sources: the change calendar, Activity Log alerts, peers. Make the gap visible: "We learned about Tuesday's launch from a page. Can we get on the launch list?"

Document: each late discovery and its impact. Escalate when: it causes an incident or a missed commitment.

The Meeting After the Meeting

Signs: decisions agreed in the room get reversed later, in a conversation you weren't part of.

What it's for: overturning outcomes without open debate.

Counter: send a written decision recap within the hour ("Decision: X. Owner: Y. Shout by 5 p.m. if I've got this wrong"). Pre-wire the key people yourself, and ask your boss to confirm big decisions in writing.

Document: recaps and reversals. Escalate when: reversals repeat on the same topic.

Triangulation

Signs: someone tells you what a third person "really thinks" about you, or plays two of you against each other privately.

What it's for: control through division and anxiety.

Counter: go to the source directly and calmly: "I heard you have concerns about the SLO plan. I'd rather hear them from you." Refuse to relay messages yourself.

Document: only if it becomes a pattern. Escalate when: it damages working relationships across teams.

Sabotage by Delay (Slow-Walking)

Signs: agreed actions keep slipping without explanation. Every request "needs more analysis." "Yes" never turns into a date.

What it's for: killing an initiative without openly opposing it.

Counter: put every commitment in writing with an owner and a date. After the second slip, move it to their manager: "This has slipped twice. Is it a priority conflict we should decide together?" Use decision deadlines.

Document: commitments and slips with dates. Escalate when: the second slip on anything important.

The Weaponized Postmortem

Signs: an RCA or review is used to build a case against a person or team. Leading questions, cherry-picked timeline, "human error" as the conclusion.

What it's for: blame with a process stamp on it.

Counter: hold the blameless ground rule out loud. Insist on the evidence-based timeline and the four defenses. Run it with the Postmortem Meeting playbook, and offer to facilitate.

Document: drafts, comments, and evidence. Escalate when: the review is used in performance conversations.

Public Undermining

Signs: you're interrupted, contradicted, or talked down to in meetings with senior people present. Your points get repeated by someone else and credited to them.

What it's for: lowering your standing where it counts.

Counter: stay calm and specific: "Let me finish the point. It matters for the decision." Reclaim credit: "Yes, that's the proposal I made a minute ago. Let's go with it." Raise the pattern privately with the person afterwards, then with your boss if it continues.

Document: dates, meetings, witnesses. Escalate when: it continues after a private conversation.

The "Not Technical" Label

Signs: your points get dismissed with "you wouldn't understand," or engineers go around you to your boss.

What it's for: excluding you from decisions you're accountable for.

Counter: don't argue the label. Make it irrelevant: ask the failure-mode question nobody else asked, bring the numbers, run the incident well. See When You're Tested or Dismissed.

Document: only if it becomes exclusion from decisions. Escalate when: you're cut out of decisions you own.

Divide and Conquer: SRE vs the NOC

Signs: others play SRE and the NOC against each other ("your NOC is the problem," "SRE never helps us"), so neither team has a united voice.

What it's for: weakening both teams, so neither can push back on the pods.

Counter: you lead both. Present them as one function in public, settle disagreements privately, and share credit across both.

Document: incidents where blame was shifted between the teams. Escalate when: it affects either team's resourcing or reviews.

Isolating the Leader

Signs: your team members get pulled into side conversations or decisions without you, or are encouraged to go around you.

What it's for: weakening you by separating you from your team. This is the destructive reading of Law 42, "Strike the shepherd."

Counter: stay close to your team in 1:1s, tell them openly that you want to know about side requests, and agree with your peers that requests go through you or with you copied.

Document: bypass incidents. Escalate when: your authority over your team's priorities is undermined.

What Actually Happens

You confront it emotionally and become the problem.
Counter: calm, factual, written. Emotion is what lets the other side reframe you.
You wait too long, until the pattern has already damaged you.
Counter: the same-day log means you can escalate on the third instance with evidence.
HR treats it as a "personality conflict."
Counter: bring dated facts and business impact, not feelings. "Three reversed decisions delayed the ownership program by six weeks."
