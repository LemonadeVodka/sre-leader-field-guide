Reliability Field Guide

The Tactics Repertoire

39 political tactics in 8 groups, each with its source, how it works, where it works for you, its backfire risk, and how to counter it.

How to Read This

Each tactic shows its source, how it works, where it works for you as a central SRE leader, its backfire risk, and what to do when it's used on you. Run the Risk Ledger before using anything rated Medium or High. Tactics designed to harm a specific person are on When It's Used on You, for defense.

Information

Whoever controls what's known, when, and how controls the decision.

Say Less Than Necessary

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 4: “Always say less than necessary”
How it works
In contested settings, the more you say, the more you commit to and the more there is to attack. Short, deliberate statements make you look more certain, and silence pulls the other side into revealing their position.
Where it works for you
In an escalation call with a resistant pod: state the impact and the ask in two sentences, then stop. Let them propose the date. Whatever they offer first is usually more than you'd have asked for.
Backfire risk
Low Overdone, it reads as evasive or cold. Keep it for negotiations, not for your team.
When it's used on you
Ask closed, specific questions ("Is that a yes for Friday?"), and summarize their silence in writing: "I take it there's no date yet. I'll note that."

Be the Source of the Numbers

Source
Pfeffer, Power: power flows from controlling resources others need. Also The 48 Laws of Power, Law 3: “Conceal your intentions”
How it works
Become the place where reliability data lives: the SLO dashboard, pages per shift, the top offenders, time to owner engaged. Whoever produces the numbers frames them, and decides when they surface.
Where it works for you
You own the monthly reliability report. You choose which three numbers lead, and which pod's story gets told first.
Backfire risk
Medium If people think you're hiding or spinning data, you lose trust fast. Never fake a number; choose emphasis, not facts.
When it's used on you
Ask for raw-data access, and publish your own numbers from the same source. "Here's what App Insights shows for the same window."

Deliver the Bad News Yourself, First

Source
Common political practice (inoculation); Greene, The 33 Strategies of War, on seizing the initiative
How it works
The first credible account of a bad event becomes the reference version. If you deliver it, with your framing and a plan, others have to argue against your version.
Where it works for you
Tell your boss about the outage, the impact, and your three actions before the pod manager or an exec frames it as "SRE missed it."
Backfire risk
Low Only risky if your account turns out to be wrong. Stick to facts you can prove, and label what's still unknown.
When it's used on you
If someone beat you to it, don't fight their frame head on. Add evidence: "Adding to that, the timeline shows…"

Strategic Ambiguity

Source
Greene, The 33 Strategies of War; also The 48 Laws of Power, Law 3: “Conceal your intentions”
How it works
Keep a commitment or plan deliberately unspecific to keep options open until you have more information or leverage.
Where it works for you
When asked to commit SRE to a new service before the PRR is done: "We'll support it as soon as it's ready," without defining "ready" until you've checked.
Backfire risk
Medium People notice vagueness. Use it with peers and execs on unsettled issues, never with your own team about their work, or they'll stop trusting your word.
When it's used on you
Pin it down: owner, date, and what "done" means. "What specifically would 'ready' mean?"

Let Them Underestimate You

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 21: “Play a sucker to catch a sucker: seem dumber than your mark”
How it works
If people think you're not technical, they explain more, guard less, and reveal the weak spots in their own proposals. Naive-sounding questions often expose more than expert ones.
Where it works for you
In a design review: "Walk me through what happens if the provider is down. I want to make sure I follow." The gaps appear in their answer.
Backfire risk
Medium Overdone, it reinforces the "not technical" label. Pair it with one sharp, informed point per meeting.
When it's used on you
When someone plays dumb with you, ask them to explain their own proposal in full detail, in writing.

Timing

The same move can win or lose depending on when you make it.

Pre-Wire the Decision

Source
Pfeffer, 7 Rules of Power; standard organizational politics
How it works
Meet each key stakeholder one-on-one before the meeting. Resolve objections privately, so the meeting ratifies a decision that's already made.
Where it works for you
Before the monthly review where you'll propose an error budget freeze: align the pod manager and your boss separately the day before.
Backfire risk
Low If someone finds out they were the only one not consulted, they'll feel ambushed. Pre-wire everyone who can block it.
When it's used on you
If you suspect it's been pre-wired against you: "Has this been discussed already? I'd like a day to review before we decide."

Use the Crisis Window

Source
Machiavelli, The Prince, on seizing opportunity; The 48 Laws of Power, Law 35: “Master the art of timing”
How it works
Attention and willingness to spend are highest right after a visible failure. Asks that were impossible last month become easy.
Where it works for you
In the exec readout after a Sev 1: "Two of our last three Sev 1s stalled on ownership. I'd like your sponsorship for the ownership program."
Backfire risk
Medium If it looks opportunistic or tone-deaf (pitching while users are still down), it backfires. Ask after mitigation, tied directly to the incident.
When it's used on you
When someone uses a crisis for a land grab, insist on facts first: "Let's see what the RCA shows before we reorganize."

Let a Documented Risk Land

Source
Organizational politics; related to The 48 Laws of Power, Law 9: “Win through your actions, never through argument”
How it works
After you've warned in writing and been overruled, stop silently absorbing the consequences. When the risk materializes, the record speaks for you.
Where it works for you
A pod declines a quota alert you recommended, in writing. You stop manually watching the quota every afternoon. When throttling hits, the RCA shows the declined recommendation.
Backfire risk
High Users get hurt, so never withhold incident response, never sabotage, and only do this after proper escalation. Done wrong, it looks like you let things fail on purpose.
When it's used on you
If someone's warning lands in your lap, answer it in writing with a decision, an owner, and a date, or an explicit risk acceptance by someone senior.

Control the Agenda and the Clock

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 31: “Control the options: get others to play with the cards you deal”
How it works
Whoever sets the agenda decides what gets discussed, in what order, and for how long. Items at the end of an agenda get rushed or dropped.
Where it works for you
Put "open RCA actions by pod" first in the monthly review, while senior people are fresh and present.
Backfire risk
Low Blatant agenda games get noticed. Keep it to legitimate prioritization.
When it's used on you
Submit your agenda items in advance, in writing, and ask for a fixed time slot.

Wait Them Out

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 35: “Master the art of timing”
How it works
When your position is weak, don't force it. Wait for the conditions to change: a new leader, a budget cycle, an incident.
Where it works for you
A pod manager blocks pod on-call. You park it, collect stuck-time data for two months, and raise it again at planning time with numbers.
Backfire risk
Low You can miss the window. Set yourself a date to revisit.
When it's used on you
If someone is stalling you, set a decision deadline: "If there's no decision by Friday, I'll take it to [boss] at Monday's review."

Alliances

Power in organizations is mostly relationships. Build them before you need them.

Build the Coalition Before You Need It

Source
Pfeffer, Power, on building networks
How it works
Invest in relationships when you don't need anything: help in their incidents, share data, make introductions. When you do need support, it's already there.
Where it works for you
Offer a pod manager a clean alert-noise analysis for free. Three months later, they're the first to back your ownership program.
Backfire risk
Low None, if it's genuine. If it's obviously transactional, people discount it.
When it's used on you
Map who's allied with whom before contested decisions, and build your own coalition on that issue.

Get a Sponsor Above You

Source
Pfeffer, Power, on relationships with powerful people
How it works
A visible backer one or two levels up multiplies your influence. One line from them does more than ten of your meetings.
Where it works for you
Get your boss to send a note to pod managers: "The ownership program is a priority for us this quarter."
Backfire risk
Medium Your fortunes become tied to theirs. If they leave or fall out of favor, you need other backers.
When it's used on you
If your opponent has a sponsor, engage the sponsor directly with data rather than fighting their proxy.

Use a Credible Messenger

Source
Cialdini, Influence (authority); a borrowed-credibility version of The 48 Laws of Power, Law 7: “Get others to do the work for you”
How it works
Some audiences trust a senior engineer more than a manager. Let the respected technical voice deliver the technical case.
Where it works for you
Have your strongest SRE present the burn-rate alerting design to the pods. You frame the business case afterwards.
Backfire risk
Low As long as you credit them publicly. Taking the credit yourself destroys the relationship.
When it's used on you
Ask the messenger questions directly, and see whether they own the content or are reciting it.

Win the Informal Leader

Source
Adapted from The 48 Laws of Power, Law 42: “Strike the shepherd and the sheep will scatter”. Used constructively: recruit the shepherd
How it works
Every group has an informal leader whose opinion others follow. Convince them, and the group follows.
Where it works for you
The most respected engineer in a pod is skeptical of pod on-call. Spend an hour with them. If they agree, the pod does.
Backfire risk
Low None, if you persuade rather than pressure.
When it's used on you
If someone is working on your team's informal leader, talk to that person directly and early.

Be Easy to Say Yes To

Source
Cialdini, Influence (liking)
How it works
People say yes more to people they like: shared goals, genuine compliments, cooperation, familiarity.
Where it works for you
Start pod syncs with something that went well for them before your asks.
Backfire risk
Low Fake warmth is obvious. Make it real or skip it.
When it's used on you
When someone is very charming before a big ask, separate the ask from the person: "Let me look at this on its own merits."

Framing & Narrative

How a situation is described often decides how it's resolved.

Frame It as Their Priority

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 13: “When asking for help, appeal to people's self-interest”
How it works
Present your ask in terms of what the other person already cares about: their launch, their metric, their boss's priority. Not your needs.
Where it works for you
Not "SRE needs SLOs." Instead: "An SLO gives your team a clear line for when you can ship faster, and protects the Q3 launch."
Backfire risk
Low If the link is a stretch, it sounds manipulative. Make the benefit real.
When it's used on you
Ask, "What problem does this solve for my team?" and wait for a specific answer.

Own the Story

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 6: “Court attention at all costs” (adapted); Greene, The 33 Strategies of War
How it works
Be first with a clear, written summary: the incident recap, the meeting notes, the monthly report. The written record becomes the version people remember.
Where it works for you
Send the incident summary within the hour, and the RCA share-out the same day.
Backfire risk
Low Only if your summary is biased. Keep it factual, and others will start relying on yours.
When it's used on you
Reply to a slanted summary quickly, in writing, with corrections and evidence.

Name the Objection Before They Do

Source
Voss, Never Split the Difference (labeling; the accusation audit)
How it works
Say the other side's likely objections or feelings out loud first. It defuses them and signals you understand.
Where it works for you
"This probably feels like SRE pushing work onto your team, and with your launch next month that's the last thing you need." Then make the ask.
Backfire risk
Low Done mechanically, it sounds scripted. Use your own words.
When it's used on you
Notice when your objections are named for you. Respond to the substance, not the empathy.

Turn Pain Into Numbers

Source
Pfeffer, Power; standard business-case practice
How it works
Opinions can be argued with; well-sourced numbers mostly can't. Translate pain into hours, pages, users affected, and money.
Where it works for you
"This issue cost 26 engineer-hours last month, about 15% of one engineer, and paged on-call 31 times."
Backfire risk
Medium If your numbers are challenged and found shaky, your credibility takes a hit. Show the source and method.
When it's used on you
Ask for the method and the raw data, and check whether the time window was cherry-picked.

Show Others Already Do It

Source
Cialdini, Influence (social proof and authority)
How it works
People follow what peers and respected authorities do. "Other teams already do this" and "this is how Google runs SRE" lower resistance.
Where it works for you
"The Search pod already has an SLO and cut their pages by half." Or cite the SRE Workbook chapter.
Backfire risk
Low If the comparison doesn't hold up, it backfires. Use real examples.
When it's used on you
"That's them. What's different about our situation?"

Leverage & Reciprocity

Trades, obligations, and what others need from you.

Give First

Source
Cialdini, Influence (reciprocity)
How it works
Help, information, or credit given first creates a feeling of obligation that makes your later ask easier.
Where it works for you
Clean up a pod's alert noise for them, then ask for their PagerDuty escalation policy and a first-15-minutes runbook.
Backfire risk
Low None if genuine. Keeping score out loud ruins it.
When it's used on you
Accept favors knowingly. Don't let a small favor buy a big concession: "Thanks, and that's separate from this decision."

Make the Trade-Off Public: “Yes, If…”

Source
Negotiation practice; The 48 Laws of Power, Law 9: “Win through your actions, never through argument”
How it works
Never just say no. State the cost in writing so the requester has to own the trade-off.
Where it works for you
"We can support the launch next week if the reliability project slips a month. Your call. Please confirm in writing."
Backfire risk
Low It's hard to argue with. Just make sure the trade-off you name is real.
When it's used on you
When it's used on you, decide explicitly and document the decision. Don't leave it hanging.

Get a Small Public Commitment

Source
Cialdini, Influence (commitment and consistency)
How it works
People act consistently with what they've said publicly. Get a small, specific commitment in front of others, then hold them to it.
Where it works for you
In the postmortem: "So we agree the target is under 5 pages a week from this alert?" Get the nod, then put it in the recap.
Backfire risk
Low If it feels like a trap, people stop agreeing to anything in public.
When it's used on you
Avoid casual public commitments: "Let me come back to you on the number."

Use What They Need From You

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 11: “Learn to keep people dependent on you”
How it works
Notice what others need from SRE (PRR sign-off, NOC coverage, launch support) and make it conditional on what you need.
Where it works for you
"We'll take on NOC coverage for the new feature once the PRR items are done: runbook, alerts, PagerDuty route."
Backfire risk
High Easily seen as holding launches hostage. Tie it to written standards your boss has agreed, not to personal favors.
When it's used on you
Escalate to shared leadership with the business impact, or meet the written standard so the leverage disappears.

Become the Hub

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 11: “Learn to keep people dependent on you”
How it works
Own something everyone needs in a crisis: the incident process, the NOC, the reliability data. People who need you consult you.
Where it works for you
Your team runs every major incident and the monthly reliability review. Nobody plans a launch without talking to you.
Backfire risk
Medium Become a bottleneck and you burn out, and get seen as hoarding. Be indispensable as a function, not as a person: document and delegate.
When it's used on you
If someone makes themselves the only path, build an alternative: documentation, a second contact, direct access.

Visibility & Credit

Work nobody sees doesn't build power.

Make Your Team's Work Visible

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 6: “Court attention at all costs” (applied to your team)
How it works
Prevention is invisible by default. Make it visible on purpose: weekly updates, demos, before-and-after numbers, named shout-outs.
Where it works for you
The weekly update to your boss always includes one win, with names and numbers: "Ana's runbook cut NOC escalations on queue alerts to zero."
Backfire risk
Low Bragging about trivia wears thin. Keep it to real outcomes.
When it's used on you
If others' visibility crowds you out, get on the same stage: offer a slot in their review to present SRE's part.

Make Your Boss Look Good

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 1: “Never outshine the master”
How it works
Bosses back people who make them look good and don't show them up. Let them present wins upward, and never correct them in public.
Where it works for you
Give your boss the one-page reliability summary before their staff meeting, so they present it.
Backfire risk
Medium Overdone, you become invisible above your boss. Make sure at least one exec knows your work directly.
When it's used on you
If a report is outshining you, back them. Their success reflects on you. Insecurity is the real risk.

Credit Generously, Sideways

Source
Pfeffer, Power (networks and reputation)
How it works
Publicly credit pods and peers for fixes. People cooperate with leaders who make them look good.
Where it works for you
"Thanks to the Assistant pod for the retry fix. Pages are down 80%." Sent to their manager and yours.
Backfire risk
Low Almost none, and it compounds over time.
When it's used on you
If someone credits you lavishly before an ask, enjoy it, then judge the ask on its merits.

Win With Results, Not Debate

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 9: “Win through your actions, never through argument”
How it works
Arguments create resentment even when you win. A small, visible result changes minds without anyone having to admit they were wrong.
Where it works for you
Instead of debating SLOs with skeptics, pilot one on a single journey and show the monthly report.
Backfire risk
Low Results take time. Pick fast pilots.
When it's used on you
Ask opponents for their pilot too: "Let's both try it for a month."

Positioning

Where you stand, and how you're seen, before any specific fight.

Change the Substance, Not the Vocabulary

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 38: “Think as you like but behave like others”
How it works
New ideas get resisted when they sound foreign. Use the organization's language and rituals while changing what actually happens.
Where it works for you
If the culture says "launch checklist," call the PRR that. If they love their weekly ops meeting, put the SLO review inside it.
Backfire risk
Low Very low. The only risk is looking like you're dressing up old ideas, so make sure the substance really changes.
When it's used on you
When someone rebrands to slip a change past you, ask what's actually different.

Plan All the Way to the End

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 29: “Plan all the way to the end”
How it works
Know the end state you want (pods on-call for their code, SRE as a partner, not a dumping ground) and choose each move for whether it leads there.
Where it works for you
Don't win an argument about one alert in a way that poisons the relationship you'll need for the ownership program.
Backfire risk
Low Rigid plans break. Re-plan quarterly.
When it's used on you
Ask yourself where the other side's moves are heading, not just what they want today.

Pick Your Fights

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 23: “Concentrate your forces”
How it works
Political capital is limited. Spend it on one or two issues that matter most, and concede the rest gracefully.
Where it works for you
This quarter: ownership and stuck incidents. Let the dashboard tooling debate go.
Backfire risk
Low You'll lose some small battles. That's the point.
When it's used on you
When someone opens many small fights with you, ask which one actually matters to them.

Make Your Time Scarce

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 16: “Use absence to increase respect and honor”; Cialdini (scarcity)
How it works
Being always available lowers the value of your attention. Being selective raises it.
Where it works for you
Stop attending every pod standup. Send your SRE lead, and show up for the decisions.
Backfire risk
Medium Overdone, you look aloof or out of touch. Stay highly available to your own team.
When it's used on you
If someone is "too busy" to engage, go through their calendar owner or their boss with a specific, short ask.

Shape Your Own Reputation

Source
The 48 Laws of Power, Law 25: “Re-create yourself”
How it works
Reputations form by default unless you shape them. Decide what you want to be known for, and make deliberate, visible moves toward it.
Where it works for you
From "the IT Ops person" to "the person who owns the reliability story": monthly SLO reports, sharp questions in design reviews, clean incident leadership.
Backfire risk
Low A mismatch between image and substance gets exposed. Back it with results.
When it's used on you
If someone is labeling you ("not technical"), don't argue the label. Replace it with a stronger one through visible results.

Pressure & Negotiation

Moves for when you need a decision, not a discussion.

The Fait Accompli

Source
Greene, The 33 Strategies of War, Strategy 29: “Take small bites” (the fait accompli)
How it works
Do something small within your own authority before asking permission. Once it exists and works, it becomes the status quo.
Where it works for you
Start publishing pages-per-shift and time-to-owner-engaged in your weekly update without asking. Soon everyone expects it.
Backfire risk
Medium Outside your authority, it reads as going rogue. Stay strictly within your remit.
When it's used on you
Name it and reopen it formally: "I don't think we decided this. Let's put it on the agenda."

Anchor High

Source
Negotiation research (anchoring); Cialdini (contrast)
How it works
The first number sets the reference point. Ask for more than you expect, with a credible rationale, and settle where you wanted to be.
Where it works for you
Ask for three engineers for the ownership program, backed by stuck-time data. Settle at two.
Backfire risk
Medium An absurd anchor costs credibility. Keep it defensible.
When it's used on you
Don't respond to their anchor. Reset with your own data-based number: "Our data supports about X."

Make Them Solve Your Problem

Source
Voss, Never Split the Difference (calibrated questions)
How it works
"How" and "what" questions make the other side work on your constraint instead of defending their position.
Where it works for you
Asked to take on a launch with no runbook: "How am I supposed to support this at 3 a.m. without a runbook?" Then wait.
Backfire risk
Low Overused, it sounds like a technique. Mix it with plain statements.
When it's used on you
When it's used on you, answer with your constraints and a question back.

Force a Decision Point

Source
Standard negotiation practice; The 48 Laws of Power, Law 35: “Master the art of timing”
How it works
Open-ended discussions favor whoever benefits from delay. A clear deadline with a default forces the decision.
Where it works for you
"If I don't hear otherwise by Friday, we'll proceed with the escalation ladder as written."
Backfire risk
Medium Artificial deadlines get called out. Tie them to something real: a launch, a review, a quarter.
When it's used on you
"What happens if we miss that date?" Don't accept a deadline that only serves them.

Use Silence

Source
Voss, Never Split the Difference; The 48 Laws of Power, Law 4: “Always say less than necessary”
How it works
After making an ask or hearing a weak offer, stay silent. Most people fill the silence with concessions or information.
Where it works for you
Pod manager: "Maybe next quarter." You: [silence]. Them: "…though we could probably do the alert part this sprint."
Backfire risk
Low None, if used sparingly.
When it's used on you
Get comfortable with silence too. Repeat your position calmly instead of filling it.
