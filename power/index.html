Power & Politics: The Real Playbook

How power actually works for a central SRE leader without authority over the pods, the Risk Ledger to run before any move, and the books it draws on.

Why This Section Exists

Being right isn't enough to get things done in an organization. A central SRE leader has responsibility for reliability without authority over the teams that own the code. That gap is filled by power: information, relationships, timing, reputation, and leverage. This section is a candid repertoire of how power actually works, drawn from the classic books on it, applied to your world, with the backfire risk of each move stated honestly.

Where Your Power Comes From

You don't control the pods' code or roadmaps. You do control, or can build, these five sources:

SourceWhat it isHow to build it
InformationYou know what's breaking, how often, and what it costsOwn the SLO dashboards, the top offenders, and the monthly reliability report
RelationshipsPeople who'll back you when it's contestedHelp peers in their incidents; credit them publicly; get a sponsor above you
TimingKnowing when an ask will landAsk right after visible incidents, and at planning cycles, with data ready
ReputationWhat people assume about you before you speakSharp questions, clean incident leadership, results shown with numbers
DependencyWhat others need from youIncident process, NOC coverage, PRR sign-off, the reliability story

The Risk Ledger

Run this check before using any tactic rated Medium or High. It's practical, not moral: these are the questions that decide whether a move helps your career or ends it.

  1. If this were exposed, would I survive it? Imagine it described in a meeting with your boss and HR present.
  2. Is it reversible? Can you undo it, or recover if it fails?
  3. Who loses, and will they remember? Today's opponent is next year's peer, or your boss.
  4. Does it create HR or legal exposure? Anything involving deception about facts, a person's reputation, or retaliation: stop.
  5. Does it cost my team's trust? Your people watch how you play. Moves you'd hide from them usually cost more than they win.

Two or more worrying answers: choose a different tactic. There almost always is one.

What Actually Happens

You read the 48 Laws and try ten of them in a week.
Counter: people notice a sudden change in behavior. Pick two tactics that fit your style, and use them consistently.
The tactic works, and you lose a relationship you needed.
Counter: that's what the third Risk Ledger question is for. Ask it every time.
You assume everyone else is playing games.
Counter: most friction is overload and misaligned priorities, not schemes. Check the signs before concluding it's personal.
