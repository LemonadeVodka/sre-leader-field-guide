Reliability Field Guide

Political Playbooks for an SRE Leader

Eight situations you'll face, each with a sequence of moves, the risks, and what winning actually looks like.

How These Work

Each playbook combines tactics from the repertoire into a sequence of moves for a real situation you're likely to face. Each shows the risks, and what winning actually looks like (it's rarely total victory).

A Pod Refuses to Own a Recurring Issue

  1. Turn pain into numbers: pages, hours, and users affected per month, from the top offenders data. (Turn Pain Into Numbers)
  2. Give first: offer an analysis of their alert noise, or a quick fix SRE can do. (Give First)
  3. Frame it as their priority: "This is eating time your engineers need for the launch." (Frame It as Their Priority)
  4. Make the trade-off public in writing: "We can keep absorbing it, and here's what it costs us. Your call." (Yes, If…)
  5. If still no: stop silently absorbing it, after documenting the warning. Bring it to the monthly review with your sponsor present. (Let a Documented Risk Land, Sponsor)

Risks: escalating too early burns the relationship. Letting the risk land must never mean withholding incident response. Winning looks like: the issue is in the pod's plan with an owner and a date, and they still take your calls.

A Peer Takes Credit for SRE's Save

  1. Tell your story first next time: wins in your weekly update, with names and evidence. (Own the Story)
  2. Reclaim it calmly in public: "Glad it worked. Ana built that fix. Ana, anything to add?" (Credit Theft)
  3. Talk to the peer privately: "I want to make sure my team gets visibility for their work. Can we credit them in your updates?"
  4. If it repeats: make sure your boss already knows the facts from your updates.

Risks: looking petty if it's a one-off. Winning looks like: your team's name is on its work, and the peer credits you by default.

An Exec Blames SRE for an Outage the Pod Caused

  1. Deliver the facts yourself, first, with the timeline and three actions. Blame the system, not the pod. (Bad News First)
  2. Use the evidence-based timeline to show the trigger and what SRE did. "The trigger was the 13:52 change. SRE escalated in 3 minutes."
  3. Turn it into an ask: "What would have prevented this is X. I need your support for it." (Use the Crisis Window)
  4. Brief the pod manager before the exec does, so they hear the systemic framing from you. (Pre-Wire)

Risks: pointing at the pod makes an enemy. Keep it systemic. Winning looks like: the exec's takeaway is "we need X," not "SRE failed," and the pod sees you as fair.

Your Boss Surprises You in a Meeting

  1. Don't fight it in the room: "Good question. Let me get you accurate numbers by 3." (Saying "I Don't Know")
  2. Deliver early, in writing, with a plan.
  3. Privately afterwards: "I'd love a heads-up on topics like that, so I can bring data. Can I send you a weekly one-pager so you're never surprised either?" (Make Your Boss Look Good)

Risks: being defensive in public. Winning looks like: your boss starts using your one-pager in their own meetings.

Competing for Headcount

  1. Collect the data a quarter early: toil %, pages per shift, time to owner engaged, stuck incidents.
  2. Build the coalition: pod managers who'd benefit say so to your boss. (Coalition)
  3. Anchor with a defensible ask tied to outcomes. (Anchor High)
  4. Pre-wire the decision-makers one-on-one before the planning meeting. (Pre-Wire)
  5. Offer a trade: "With two engineers we deliver X by Q3. With one, Y by Q4." (Yes, If…)

Risks: inflated numbers backfire for years. Winning looks like: you get what you can defend, and your numbers become the reference for next year.

The Offshore Vendor Games the Metrics

  1. Measure what matters yourself: escalation timeliness and runbook adherence on sampled tickets, not their dashboard. (Be the Source of the Numbers)
  2. Ask about incentives: "How are analysts rewarded?" (NOC Through the Vendor)
  3. Put quality into the contract at the next review, using your sampled data. (Force a Decision Point)
  4. Protect the analysts in public while holding the vendor accountable in private.

Risks: blaming individual analysts for a vendor system problem. Winning looks like: quality measures are in the SOW, and escalation timeliness improves.

You're Being Sidelined From Decisions

  1. Check the signs on Information Starvation and the Meeting After the Meeting.
  2. Ask directly to be included, tied to impact: "Reliability decisions without SRE have caused [incident]."
  3. Become the hub for something they can't do without: the reliability report, the incident process. (Become the Hub)
  4. Get your sponsor to add you to the right forums. (Sponsor)

Risks: complaining without leverage. Winning looks like: you're invited because decisions go worse without you.

A New Leader Arrives and Tests You

  1. Bring the story before they ask: a one-page summary of reliability state, risks, and your plan. (Own the Story)
  2. Learn what they care about and frame your priorities in their terms. (Frame It as Their Priority)
  3. Deliver one visible, quick win in the first 30 days. (Win With Results)
  4. Don't outshine them in public. Make their early decisions look good. (Make Your Boss Look Good)

Risks: being seen as part of the old regime. Winning looks like: they see you as the person who explains reliability to them.

What Actually Happens

The situation doesn't match any playbook cleanly.
Counter: they rarely do. Pick the closest one, run the Risk Ledger, and take the first move only. Then reassess.
You win the battle and lose the relationship.
Counter: decide in advance what "winning" means. It's usually a commitment, not a defeat.
