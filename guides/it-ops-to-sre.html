Reliability Field Guide

From IT Ops to SRE Leader

What carries over from IT Operations, what to unlearn, where your background is an advantage, and what you are and aren't expected to know.

Use This When

You're in a room full of engineers and wondering whether you belong there. Or someone has said, out loud or not, "they're not really technical." This page is the honest answer to both.

The Short Version

An SRE leader's job isn't to be the best engineer. It's to make the system and the team more reliable over time: decide what matters, get it owned, protect the people doing the work, and tell the story to leadership with data. Most of that is operations leadership, which you already know. What you need to add is fluency: enough to follow the conversation, ask the question that changes it, and not be bluffed.

What Carries Over

You've been doing a version of SRE for years under different names. Use the translations below, because engineers respond to their own vocabulary.

You know it as (IT Ops)SRE calls itWhat changes
Incident management (ITIL)Incident response, incident commandSame discipline. More emphasis on stopping the bleeding first and on blameless reviews afterwards.
Problem managementPost-incident reviews, repeat offendersRoot cause is less of a single thing. SRE looks for "contributing factors" and tracks whether actions actually get done.
Change management, CABRelease engineering, canaries, feature flags, error budgetsSafety comes from small, frequent, reversible changes, not from approval meetings. (Big difference. See Unlearn below.)
SLAsSLOs and error budgetsAn SLA is a promise to customers with penalties. An SLO is an internal target that sits tighter than the SLA, so you get warned before you break the promise.
NOC monitoring, thresholdsObservability, alerting on symptomsAlert on what users feel (slow, failing, wrong), not on every CPU spike. Fewer, better pages.
Runbooks, SOPsRunbooks, then automationA runbook step someone does by hand every week is "toil." SRE's instinct is to automate it away.
CMDB, asset ownershipService catalog, service ownershipEvery service has an owning team that carries the consequences, ideally including the pager.
Vendor managementDependency management (e.g., the model provider)Same skill: know what you depend on, what the contract says, and what happens when they fail.
24x7 shift operationsOn-call rotations, follow-the-sunSame logistics. SRE adds a hard focus on on-call load and burnout as a reliability risk.
Capacity planningCapacity planning, load managementAlmost identical. In the cloud it shows up as cost as much as hardware.

What to Unlearn

Old instinctSRE instinctWhy it matters for you
"The goal is 100% uptime.""The goal is the right amount of reliability, and the rest is budget for change."Arguing for 100% makes you look like you don't understand the trade-off. Arguing for an error budget makes you a partner.
"Approvals make change safe.""Small, tested, reversible changes make change safe."Proposing a CAB to engineers is the fastest way to get labeled "old-school IT." Ask about rollback and canaries instead.
"Tickets closed = success.""The same problem not coming back = success."Measure recurrence and pages, not ticket counts.
"Ops runs it, dev builds it.""Whoever builds it shares responsibility for running it."Your leverage with pods depends on this principle. Learn to say it calmly and often.
"Monitor everything, alert on thresholds.""Alert on user pain; dashboards are for investigating."Noisy alerts burn out your offshore team and your SREs. Fewer alerts is a win, not a risk.
"The hero who fixes it at 3 a.m. is the best engineer.""The engineer who makes 3 a.m. pages stop is the best engineer."Reward prevention visibly, or your team will optimize for heroics.

Your Unfair Advantages

  • Calm in the fire. Many engineers have never run a major incident. You've run hundreds. Running the room (impact, roles, comms, escalation) is a skill most of them don't have.
  • The user's view. You came up through support. You know what an outage feels like to the person on the other end, and that is exactly what SLOs try to measure.
  • Process that actually runs. Shift handoffs, escalation paths, runbooks people follow. Engineers often design processes that work on paper and nowhere else.
  • Running a 24x7 operation. Scheduling, coverage, fatigue, offshore coordination. This is a real discipline, and it's yours.
  • Translating for the business. You can turn "p99 latency regression" into "1 in 100 customers waited 20 seconds," which is the version leadership acts on.

Example in Practice

The moment: in a design review, a senior engineer says, a little impatiently, "It's an async retry with exponential backoff, it's fine."

The bluffing response: nodding along. You've lost the room and learned nothing.

The defensive response: "Well, I've been running ops for 15 years…" You've lost the room and made an enemy.

The leader's response: "Help me with the failure case. If the model provider is down for 20 minutes, what do users see, and does the retrying make the recovery harder?" In other words, you didn't need to know what exponential backoff is. You asked what happens to users and whether the fix makes things worse. Half the time, the room goes quiet because nobody had thought about it.

What Actually Happens

Some engineers will test you early with jargon.
Counter: don't play their game. Ask about outcomes and failure modes, which is a game they can't win by being more technical. After two or three good questions, the testing usually stops.
The "not technical" label gets used against you by a peer when it's convenient.
Counter: don't argue the label. Make it irrelevant with results and data. "Sure. Here's what the last quarter's numbers say." Make sure your boss hears your wins directly, every week, so the label doesn't define you in rooms you're not in.
You'll be tempted to overcompensate by learning to code.
Counter: a little scripting literacy is fine if you enjoy it, but it's the slowest route to credibility as a leader. Learning to steer the conversation with the right questions pays off ten times faster.
Your IT Ops background gets you pigeonholed as "just the ops person."
Counter: lead with SRE vocabulary (SLOs, error budgets, toil, ownership) and data. People can't keep calling you "just ops" when you're the one showing what reliability costs the business.
