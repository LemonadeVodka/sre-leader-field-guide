Quality & Safety Signals

"Up but wrong" incidents: how to measure answer quality and safety blocks, when a drop becomes an incident, and why prompt and model changes are deploys.

Use This When

Users say answers got worse, a prompt or model change is planned, or you're deciding what counts as an incident for an AI product. "Up but wrong" is a class of incident that traditional monitoring can't see.

Why "Up but Wrong" Is Different

A traditional service is either working or erroring. An AI assistant can return a fast, successful HTTP 200 with an answer that's unhelpful, wrong, or refused. Every infrastructure dashboard stays green while users lose trust. These incidents usually start with a change: a prompt, a model version, retrieved data, or a safety setting.

Quality Signals

SignalTypeWhat it tells you
Offline evalsBefore releaseScore a fixed test set of real-world questions against the new prompt or model, and compare with the current version. This is your quality gate.
Thumbs-down rateOnline, user feedbackA direct signal, but it lags and is noisy. Watch the trend and sudden jumps.
Refusal or "can't help" rateOnline, from responsesSpikes often mean a prompt or safety-setting change.
Fallback rateOnline, from orchestrationHow often the backup model or a degraded answer was used.
Grounding or hallucination checksOnline, sampledAutomated scoring of a sample of answers against their sources.
Task completionOnline, from Pendo or app eventsDid the user complete what they came to do, or retry and give up?

Content Filters and Safety Blocks

  • Model providers apply content filters that can block a prompt or an answer. Track the block rate as its own metric.
  • A sudden rise in blocks is an incident signal: a filter setting changed, a prompt change triggered false positives, or someone is attempting abuse.
  • Watch for prompt injection (inputs trying to override instructions) in agent features that take actions. Confirm with your security team who owns detection.

When a Quality Drop Becomes an Incident

Agree a threshold in advance, so quality problems get declared instead of discussed for a week. A starting point to agree with product:

  • Thumbs-down rate doubles against the 7-day baseline for an hour, or
  • Refusal or block rate goes above 2× baseline for 30 minutes, or
  • An offline eval regression is discovered after release.

Then run it like any incident: declare it, look for the change, roll back the prompt or model, and do an RCA.

Prompt and Model Changes Are Deploys

What Done Looks Like

  • Prompts and model versions are versioned in source control, not edited in a portal.
  • Every change passes offline evals before release.
  • Changes ship through a canary, such as 5% of traffic, with automatic rollback on quality or error signals.
  • Every change appears as a Grafana annotation.
  • Rollback is one action, and SRE can pull it as a pre-approved lever.
  • At least one quality signal is on the service dashboard, with an incident threshold.

Example in Practice

On Tuesday the thumbs-down rate rises from 4% to 9%. Errors and latency are normal, so no alert fires. The NOC notices the quality panel and declares, using the threshold. The Grafana annotation shows a model version update at 09:00. The pod rolls back by 11:30 and the rate returns to 4%. The RCA's top action: model version updates now go through the same offline eval and canary as prompt changes.

What Actually Happens

Quality is "product's problem," not reliability's.
Counter: if users can't get a useful answer, the service isn't reliable. Agree the threshold together with product.
Model versions update automatically and nobody notices.
Counter: pin model versions, and treat an upgrade as a change with evals.
Thumbs-down data sits in a product tool nobody in SRE can see.
Counter: get the signal onto the Grafana dashboard, or at least a shared daily number.
